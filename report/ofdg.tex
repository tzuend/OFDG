\section{Adapted Oscillation-Free Discontinuous Galerkin Stabilization}
\label{chap:ofdg}

This chapter describes the oscillation-free discontinuous Galerkin (OFDG)
stabilization used in this work.  The presentation deliberately separates the
mathematical operator from its realization in MFEM.  The construction combines
the projection-based damping mechanism introduced for scalar conservation laws
in~\cite{LuEtAl2021} with the scale normalization, wave-speed scaling, and exact
damping evolution developed in~\cite{PengEtAl2024}.  The extension of the
original OFDG method to hyperbolic systems is described in~\cite{LiuEtAl2022}.
The precise multidimensional sensor implemented here differs from the sensors in
those papers; the differences are stated explicitly in
\cref{sec:ofdg-relation-to-literature}.

\subsection{Mathematical formulation}
\label{sec:ofdg-mathematics}

\subsubsection{Purpose of the stabilization}

Consider a hyperbolic conservation law, or more specifically the transport problem
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t u+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(u)=0,
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  \partial_t u+\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\nabla u=0.
  \label{eq:ofdg-model-problem}
\end{equation}
Here $u=u(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ is the transported scalar quantity,
$\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega$ is position, $t$ is time,
$\boldsymbol{f}(u)$ is its physical flux, and $\boldsymbol{a}$ is a prescribed
transport velocity.  The first equation is the conservative form; the second is
the special case of linear advection.  A system of conservation laws replaces
$u$ by a vector of components.  The OFDG construction below is applied to each
component of that vector.

A discontinuous Galerkin method approximates the solution by a separate polynomial
on every mesh element.  The polynomials on two neighboring elements are not
required to agree at their common face.  This is useful because it makes the method
local and permits discontinuous physical solutions, but a high-order polynomial can
develop nonphysical over- and undershoots close to a shock or another unresolved
feature.

OFDG adds damping only to the nonconstant polynomial content of the numerical
solution.  The amount of damping is determined automatically from jumps between
neighboring element polynomials.  A smooth, well-resolved solution has small jumps
and therefore receives little damping.  A discontinuity produces large jumps and
therefore stronger damping.  The construction has three ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item local $L^2$ projections separate low- and high-order polynomial content;
  \item jumps of the solution and its physical derivatives measure a lack of
        smoothness across element faces;
  \item nonnegative damping rates combine the jump measure, element size, solution
        scale, and normal transport speed.
\end{enumerate}
The following subsections define each ingredient before combining them into the
stabilization operator.

\subsubsection{Discrete setting}

Let $\Omega\subset\R^d$ be partitioned into a mesh $\mathcal{T}_h$, and let
$\mathcal{F}_h^{\mathrm{i}}$ denote the set of interior faces.  On each element
$K\in\mathcal{T}_h$, let $V^k(K)$ be the polynomial space of degree $k\geq 1$
used by the DG discretization.  The discontinuous finite element space is
\begin{equation}
  V_h^k
  = \left\{v\in L^2(\Omega):
      v|_K\in V^k(K)\quad\text{for every }K\in\mathcal{T}_h\right\}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-space}
\end{equation}
The symbol $d$ is the spatial dimension, $K$ denotes one mesh element, and the
subscript $h$ indicates that the space is tied to a mesh with a characteristic
element size $h$.  An interior face is a full side shared by two elements; an
outer boundary side is not included in $\mathcal{F}_h^{\mathrm{i}}$.  The notation
$v|_K$ means the restriction of $v$ to $K$.  Because that restriction is chosen
independently on every element, a function in $V_h^k$ may have two different
values at an interior face.

For a system with $m$ components, the numerical solution belongs to the
vector-valued space
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{u}_h=(u_{h,1},\ldots,u_{h,m})^\mathsf{T}\in[V_h^k]^m.
\end{equation}
The present implementation treats these components independently.  It does not
perform a characteristic decomposition.

On an element $K$, choose a basis $\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^{N_k}$ of $V^k(K)$.  A local
DG polynomial is represented by
\begin{equation}
  u_h|_K(\boldsymbol{x})
  =\sum_{i=1}^{N_k}u_{K,i}\phi_i(\boldsymbol{x}).
  \label{eq:ofdg-local-expansion}
\end{equation}
The numbers $u_{K,i}$ are the local degrees of freedom.  Depending on the basis,
they may be nodal values or general expansion coefficients.  No assumption is
made here that $\{\phi_i\}$ is modal, orthogonal, or formed from Legendre
polynomials.  The word \emph{modal} used later describes the invariant
polynomial-degree subspaces selected by $L^2$ projections; it does not prescribe
the coefficient basis in which the DG framework stores $u_h$.  Both the theory
and its matrix realization below are independent of that storage basis.

For $0\leq r\leq k$, denote by
$\Pi_K^r:L^2(K)\rightarrow V^r(K)$ the element-local $L^2$ projection,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\Pi_K^r w-w,v\bigr)_K=0
  \qquad\text{for every }v\in V^r(K),
  \label{eq:ofdg-l2-projection}
\end{equation}
Here
$(p,q)_K=\int_Kpq\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}$ is the usual elementwise
$L^2$ inner product.  Equation~\eqref{eq:ofdg-l2-projection} says that the
projection error is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree at most $r$.
Equivalently, among all functions in $V^r(K)$, $\Pi_K^r w$ is the one closest to
$w$ in the root-mean-square sense over $K$.

We adopt the convention $\Pi_K^{-1}=\Pi_K^0$.  The high-pass operators used by
the stabilization are
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}=I-\Pi_K^{l-1},
  \qquad 0\leq l\leq k.
  \label{eq:ofdg-high-pass}
\end{equation}
In particular, $\mathcal{R}_K^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}_K^{(1)}=I-\Pi_K^0$.
Every $\mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}w$ has zero cell average.

The projection $\Pi_K^r w$ is the best approximation to $w$ in $V^r(K)$ in the
$L^2(K)$ norm.  It retains polynomial content up to degree $r$ and removes the
higher-order content.  Consequently,
$\mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}w=w-\Pi_K^{l-1}w$ contains precisely the part that the
$l$th damping contribution is allowed to suppress.  The convention
$\Pi_K^{-1}=\Pi_K^0$ ensures that no damping contribution changes the cell
average, which is the constant mode carrying the locally conserved quantity.

\subsubsection{Face jumps of physical derivatives}

For the multi-index
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\in\N_0^d$, define
\begin{equation}
  |\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=\sum_{j=1}^d\alpha_j,
  \qquad
  \partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
  =\partial_{x_1}^{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial_{x_d}^{\alpha_d}.
\end{equation}
The entries of $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ count how often differentiation is taken in
each coordinate direction, while $|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|$ is the total derivative
order.  In two dimensions, for example,
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1,0)$ represents $\partial_x$,
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(0,1)$ represents $\partial_y$, and
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1,1)$ represents $\partial_{xy}$.

Let $F=\partial K^-\cap\partial K^+$ be an interior face.  The sign convention
for the jump is immaterial below because only its absolute value occurs; we set
\begin{equation}
  [\![w]\!]_F=w^-|_F-w^+|_F.
  \label{eq:ofdg-jump-definition}
\end{equation}
The superscripts $-$ and $+$ are labels for the two neighboring elements, not
signs.  Thus $w^-|_F$ means: approach $F$ from inside $K^-$ and evaluate the
polynomial stored on $K^-$.  The trace $w^+|_F$ is obtained independently from
$K^+$.  Even though both traces refer to the same physical points on $F$, they
need not be equal in a DG method.

For component $c$ and total derivative order $l$, the implemented face sensor is
the sum of normalized mean absolute jumps
\begin{equation}
  J_{F,c}^{(l)}
  =\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=l}
   \frac{1}{|F|}
   \int_F
   \left|[\![\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}u_{h,c}]\!]_F\right|
   \,\mathrm{d}s.
  \label{eq:ofdg-face-jump}
\end{equation}
Every symbol in this expression has a direct interpretation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $F$ is the shared face shown in \cref{fig:ofdg-two-elements}, and $|F|$
        is its length in two dimensions or area in three dimensions;
  \item $c\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$ selects one physical solution component;
  \item $l\in\{0,\ldots,k\}$ is the total derivative order;
  \item the sum includes every coordinate derivative
        $\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ whose total order is $l$;
  \item $[\![\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}u_{h,c}]\!]_F$ is the difference
        between the two independently evaluated derivative traces; and
  \item the integral averages the magnitude of that difference over the entire
        face after division by $|F|$.
\end{itemize}
Thus $J_{F,c}^{(0)}$ measures the jump of the DG solution itself, while the
higher values of $l$ measure all physical partial derivatives of total order $l$.
For example, in two dimensions the $l=1$ contribution contains the jumps of
$\partial_xu_h$ and $\partial_yu_h$, while the $l=2$ contribution contains those
of $\partial_{xx}u_h$, $\partial_{xy}u_h$, and $\partial_{yy}u_h$.  The absolute
value is applied pointwise before integration, so positive and negative jumps
cannot cancel.  The sum over all multi-indices ensures that the sensor does not
select only one coordinate direction.

Dividing by $|F|$ turns the face integral into a face average.  Hence two faces
with the same typical jump magnitude produce comparable values even if their
physical measures differ.  In one spatial dimension a face is a point and
\eqref{eq:ofdg-face-jump} reduces to the ordinary absolute jump.  An earlier
version accumulated squared jumps and subsequently took a square root.  The
current definition uses the intended absolute jump directly; it is both simpler
and cheaper to evaluate.

As a simple scalar example, suppose the traces are constant on $F$ with
$u_h^-|_F=2$ and $u_h^+|_F=1.25$.  Then
$J_{F}^{(0)}=|2-1.25|=0.75$, independently of the length of $F$.  If the
difference varies along the face, the quadrature described later approximates
its mean absolute value instead.  This distinction matters: averaging the signed
difference could hide a nonsmooth face when positive and negative differences
cancel, whereas averaging its absolute value cannot.

Let $\boldsymbol{a}(\boldsymbol{x})$ be the prescribed transport velocity and
$\boldsymbol{n}_{K,F}$ the outward unit normal of $K$ on $F$.  The normal wave
speed used on the $K$ side of $F$ is
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{K,F}
  =\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in F}
   \left|\boldsymbol{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K,F}\right|.
  \label{eq:ofdg-normal-speed}
\end{equation}
The dot product extracts the part of the velocity crossing the face.  Tangential
transport has zero normal component and should not determine the exchange between
the two neighboring elements.  The absolute value makes the rate independent of
which side supplies the orientation of the normal.  The maximum chooses a safe
representative speed when the velocity varies along the face.  In the code this
maximum is approximated by evaluating the velocity at the face quadrature points.
When no velocity is supplied, the implementation uses $\beta_{K,F}=1$.

\subsubsection{Global scale and element damping rates}

Following the scale-invariant idea of~\cite{PengEtAl2024}, each component is
normalized by one global amplitude.  Define the componentwise extrema
\begin{equation}
  u_{\min,c}=\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_h}\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in K}u_{h,c}(\boldsymbol{x}),
  \qquad
  u_{\max,c}=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_h}\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in K}u_{h,c}(\boldsymbol{x}).
  \label{eq:ofdg-global-extrema}
\end{equation}
The global mean and amplitude are
\begin{align}
  \overline{u}_{h,c}
  &=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_\Omega u_{h,c}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},
  \label{eq:ofdg-global-mean}\\
  A_c
  &=\max\left\{
      \overline{u}_{h,c}-u_{\min,c},
      u_{\max,c}-\overline{u}_{h,c}
    \right\}
   =\left\|u_{h,c}-\overline{u}_{h,c}\right\|_{L^\infty(\Omega)}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-global-scale}
\end{align}
If $A_c=0$, all damping rates for component $c$ are defined to be zero.  For
$A_c>0$, introduce
\begin{equation}
  C_{k,l}=\frac{2l+1}{2(2k-1)l!},
  \qquad 0\leq l\leq k.
  \label{eq:ofdg-sensor-constant}
\end{equation}
The damping rate accumulated for element $K$, component $c$, and derivative
order $l$ is
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{K,c}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)
  =C_{k,l}\frac{h_K^{l-1}}{A_c}
   \sum_{\substack{F\subset\partial K\\F\in\mathcal{F}_h^{\mathrm{i}}}}
   \beta_{K,F}J_{F,c}^{(l)}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-damping-rate}
\end{equation}

For convenient reference, the complete definition is collected below as one
system:
\begin{align}
  \beta_{K,F}
  &=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in F}
    \left|\boldsymbol{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot
           \boldsymbol{n}_{K,F}\right|,
  \notag\\
  \delta_{K,c}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)
  &=\frac{2l+1}{2(2k-1)} \frac{h_K^{l-1}}{l!}
    \sum_{\substack{F\subset\partial K\\F\in\mathcal{F}_h^{\mathrm{i}}}}
    \beta_{K,F} \frac{\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=l}
   \frac{1}{|F|}
   \int_F
   \left|[\![\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}u_{h,c}]\!]_F\right|
   \,\mathrm{d}s}{\left\|u_{h,c}-\overline{u}_{h,c}\right\|_{L^\infty(\Omega)}}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-damping-rate-expanded}
\end{align}
The symbols can be read as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $K$ is the element that receives damping, and $\partial K$ is its boundary;
  \item the face sum runs only over interior faces of $K$, because only those
        faces possess two DG traces;
  \item $k$ is the polynomial degree of the DG space and
        $l\in\{0,\ldots,k\}$ is the derivative order whose jump is being measured;
  \item $c$ selects one solution component;
  \item $h_K$ is a characteristic length of element $K$; and
  \item $\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{K,F}$ is the component of the
        transport velocity that crosses the face.
\end{itemize}

The factor $h_K^{l-1}$ consists of the
$h_K^l$ derivative scaling of the sensor and the additional $h_K^{-1}$ rate in
the damping equation.  Consequently, $\delta_{K,c}^{(l)}$ has the dimensions of
an inverse time.  Equation~\eqref{eq:ofdg-damping-rate} is the exact coefficient
computed by the current code; in the implementation it is stored under the name
\texttt{sigma\_elem}.

The terms in~\eqref{eq:ofdg-damping-rate} have distinct roles.  The face sum
collects information from every interior neighbor of $K$.  The jump
$J_{F,c}^{(l)}$ has the physical units of the solution divided by length to the
power $l$.  Multiplication by $h_K^{l-1}$ and by the speed $\beta_{K,F}$ converts
it to the units of the solution divided by time.  Division by $A_c$ leaves an
inverse-time damping rate.  A larger jump, a faster wave, or a smaller element
therefore increases the rate at which unresolved polynomial content is removed.

For a concrete scalar example, take degree $k=2$, derivative order $l=0$, one
contributing face, $h_K=0.1$, $A=1$, normal speed $\beta=2$, and mean absolute
solution jump $J=0.3$.  Since
$C_{2,0}=1/[2(2\cdot2-1)]=1/6$, the resulting contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \delta_K^{(0)}
  =\frac{1}{6}\frac{0.1^{-1}}{1}(2)(0.3)=1.
\end{equation}
If time is measured in seconds, this is a damping rate of one per second.  A
second interior face would contribute another nonnegative term to the same rate.

The normalization makes the rates invariant under a rescaling and shift of a
component.  More precisely, away from the zero-amplitude threshold, replacing
$u_{h,c}$ by $\lambda u_{h,c}+b$, with $\lambda\neq 0$, leaves every
$\delta_{K,c}^{(l)}$ unchanged.  Multiplying the transport velocity by a positive
factor multiplies the rates by the same factor, so the product of a damping time
and a damping rate is unchanged under the corresponding rescaling of physical
time.

\subsubsection{Projection-based damping operator}

The local damping operator is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}_{K,c}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)u_{h,c}
  =\sum_{l=0}^k
   \delta_{K,c}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)
  \mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}u_{h,c}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-damping-operator}
\end{equation}
Substituting the definition of the high-pass operator gives the same formula in
a form that exposes exactly what is removed from the element polynomial:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}_{K,c}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)u_{h,c}
  =\sum_{l=0}^k
   \delta_{K,c}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)
   \left(u_{h,c}|_K-\Pi_K^{l-1}(u_{h,c}|_K)\right),
  \qquad \Pi_K^{-1}:=\Pi_K^0.
  \label{eq:ofdg-damping-operator-expanded}
\end{equation}
For each $l$, the projection reconstructs the part of the element polynomial of
degree at most $l-1$.  Subtracting it leaves only higher-degree content.  That
remainder is multiplied by the nonnegative rate associated with derivative
jumps of order $l$.  Finally, all orders are added.  The operation is entirely
local once the face-based rates have been computed: no value from a neighboring
element is needed while applying~\eqref{eq:ofdg-damping-operator-expanded}.

When OFDG is used as a semidiscrete stabilization, the ordinary DG operator is
augmented by
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\partial_t u_{h,c},v_h\bigr)_K
  =\mathcal{A}_{K,c}(\boldsymbol{u}_h;v_h)
   -\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{K,c}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)u_{h,c},v_h\bigr)_K,
  \label{eq:ofdg-semidiscrete}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_{K,c}$ denotes the unstabilized DG spatial discretization.
The function $v_h$ is an arbitrary DG test function, and
$(p,q)_K=\int_Kpq\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}$.  The minus sign in front of the
OFDG term is essential: because the operator is nonnegative, subtracting it
removes energy from nonconstant modes instead of amplifying them.
The implementation returns the coefficient vector of
$\mathcal{D}_{K,c}u_{h,c}$; the caller subtracts this vector from the DG time
derivative.

To see which polynomial content is affected, consider one term in
\eqref{eq:ofdg-damping-operator}.  The $l=0$ and $l=1$ terms both act on
$u_{h,c}-\Pi_K^0u_{h,c}$, namely everything except the cell average.  For
$l\geq2$, the $l$th term acts only on content not representable by polynomials of
degree $l-1$.  Thus a derivative jump of order $l$ is connected to damping of
polynomial modes of degree at least $l$.  The total operator is nonlinear because
the rates are recomputed from the current solution, although it is a linear
combination of projections once those rates have been frozen.

\begin{samepage}
For example, when $k=3$, the action of the four rates is:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{ccl}
    \toprule
    rate & remainder being multiplied & polynomial content affected \\
    \midrule
    $\delta_{K,c}^{(0)}$ & $u_{h,c}-\Pi_K^0u_{h,c}$ & degrees $1$, $2$, and $3$ \\
    $\delta_{K,c}^{(1)}$ & $u_{h,c}-\Pi_K^0u_{h,c}$ & degrees $1$, $2$, and $3$ \\
    $\delta_{K,c}^{(2)}$ & $u_{h,c}-\Pi_K^1u_{h,c}$ & degrees $2$ and $3$ \\
    $\delta_{K,c}^{(3)}$ & $u_{h,c}-\Pi_K^2u_{h,c}$ & degree $3$ only \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The constant, degree-zero part never appears in a remainder and is therefore
never damped.  This is the algebraic reason that the element average is
preserved.
\end{samepage}

The damping term is locally conservative because
\begin{equation}
  \int_K\mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}w\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
  =\int_K\left(w-\Pi_K^{l-1}w\right)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0
  \qquad\text{for all }l.
  \label{eq:ofdg-local-conservation}
\end{equation}
For $l=0$, the already stated convention $\Pi_K^{-1}=\Pi_K^0$ is used.  The
integral vanishes because an $L^2$ projection onto a space containing constants
preserves the element integral of the projected function.

It is also dissipative.  Orthogonality of the $L^2$ projection and nonnegativity
of the rates give
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\mathcal{D}_{K,c}u_{h,c},u_{h,c}\bigr)_K
  =\sum_{l=0}^k\delta_{K,c}^{(l)}
   \left\|u_{h,c}-\Pi_K^{l-1}u_{h,c}\right\|_{L^2(K)}^2
  \geq 0.
  \label{eq:ofdg-dissipation}
\end{equation}
These two identities are direct consequences of the implemented projection
operator and do not depend on the particular face sensor.

\subsubsection{Exact decay by polynomial-degree shells}
\label{sec:ofdg-exact-decay}

The same damping operator can be applied in a separate pseudo-time step.  Freeze
the rates at an input state $\boldsymbol{u}_h$ and solve, independently on each
element and for each component,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial w_{K,c}}{\partial s}
  +\sum_{l=0}^k\delta_{K,c}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)
   \mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}w_{K,c}=0,
  \qquad
  w_{K,c}(0)=u_{h,c}|_K.
  \label{eq:ofdg-decay-equation}
\end{equation}
Define the mutually orthogonal polynomial shells
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Q}_K^{(0)}=\Pi_K^0,
  \qquad
  \mathcal{Q}_K^{(j)}=\Pi_K^j-\Pi_K^{j-1},
  \quad 1\leq j\leq k,
  \label{eq:ofdg-modal-shells}
\end{equation}
and their cumulative damping rates
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{K,c}^{(j)}
  =\sum_{l=0}^j\delta_{K,c}^{(l)},
  \qquad 1\leq j\leq k.
  \label{eq:ofdg-cumulative-rate}
\end{equation}
Since a mode in the $j$th shell is contained in
$\mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}$ precisely when $l\leq j$, the exact solution at pseudo-time
$\tau$ is
\begin{equation}
  w_{K,c}(\tau)
  =\Pi_K^0u_{h,c}
   +\sum_{j=1}^k
    \exp\left(-\tau\Lambda_{K,c}^{(j)}\right)
    \mathcal{Q}_K^{(j)}u_{h,c}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-exact-decay}
\end{equation}
Eliminating the abbreviations $\Lambda_{K,c}^{(j)}$ and
$\mathcal{Q}_K^{(j)}$ gives the directly usable form
\begin{equation}
  w_{K,c}(\tau)
  =\Pi_K^0u_{h,c}
   +\sum_{j=1}^k
    \exp\left(
      -\tau\sum_{l=0}^j\delta_{K,c}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{u}_h)
    \right)
    \left(\Pi_K^j-\Pi_K^{j-1}\right)u_{h,c}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-exact-decay-expanded}
\end{equation}
Here $j$ labels a polynomial shell, $\tau\geq0$ is the duration of the damping
step, and the inner sum is the total rate acting on that shell.  The exponential
factor lies between zero and one: it equals one if the accumulated rate is zero
and approaches zero when the rate or damping time becomes large.  The first term
$\Pi_K^0u_{h,c}$ is the cell-average mode and has no exponential factor, so it is
copied unchanged.

Thus the cell average is unchanged, whereas increasingly high polynomial shells
are multiplied by nonnegative decay factors.  This is the modal-filter
interpretation emphasized in~\cite{PengEtAl2024}.  It is a statement about the
orthogonal decomposition of the polynomial space, not about the basis used to
store the coefficients: every projector and shell in
\cref{eq:ofdg-exact-decay} is applied directly in the host basis.  The exact map
avoids a time-step restriction caused solely by stiffness of the frozen damping
equation.

The coefficient-space stabilization and the exact decay are two representations
of the same operator.  Indeed,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{l=0}^k\delta_{K,c}^{(l)}\mathcal{R}_K^{(l)}
  =\sum_{j=1}^k\Lambda_{K,c}^{(j)}\mathcal{Q}_K^{(j)}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-operator-shell-equivalence}
\end{equation}
The first form is used by \texttt{ComputeStabilization}; the exponential of the
second form is used by \texttt{CompDecay}.

\subsection{Implementation decisions and details}
\label{sec:ofdg-implementation}

\subsubsection{Scope and geometric assumptions}

The implementation is a standalone MFEM header.  It assumes that the finite
element space is nonempty and discontinuous, that $k\geq 1$, and that all elements
have the same geometry, order, dimension, number of local degrees of freedom, and
component layout.  For a multicomponent field, the local degrees of freedom of
each component must form one contiguous block.  All element maps are assumed to
be affine, and the mesh must remain unchanged during the lifetime of the OFDG
object.  These conditions are preconditions rather than properties checked at run
time.
The public header exposes the OFDG constructors, stabilization, and decay only.
KXRCF is an independent optional method and is included explicitly when the
activation mask is required.

Different elements need not have the same Jacobian.  They may have different
sizes and may be translated, rotated, or affinely skewed.  General bilinear
quadrilaterals and curved elements are outside the present scope because their
inverse Jacobians vary within an element.

More explicitly, MFEM describes a physical element by a map from a standard
reference element.  The affine assumption means that this map has the form
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{x}=T_K(\boldsymbol{\xi})
  =\boldsymbol{b}_K+J_K\boldsymbol{\xi},
  \label{eq:ofdg-affine-map}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is a reference coordinate, $\boldsymbol{b}_K$ is a
translation, and the matrix $J_K$ is constant within $K$.  The matrix may differ
from one element to another.  Its scaling, rotation, and shear describe the size,
orientation, and skew of the physical element.  What the implementation requires
is therefore a constant Jacobian on each individual element, not one globally
identical Jacobian for the whole mesh.  The characteristic length $h_K$ in the
damping rate is obtained from MFEM's element-size routine for that element.

\subsubsection{Projection matrices}

Let $\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^{N_k}$ be the local degree-$k$ basis and
$\{\psi_a^{(r)}\}_{a=1}^{N_r}$ the degree-$r$ basis.  The code assembles
\begin{align}
  (M_k)_{ij}&=(\phi_i,\phi_j)_K,
  & (M_r)_{ab}&=(\psi_a^{(r)},\psi_b^{(r)})_K,\\
  (B_r)_{ai}&=(\psi_a^{(r)},\phi_i)_K.
\end{align}
The matrix representation of $I-\Pi_K^r$ in the degree-$k$ coefficient basis is
\begin{equation}
  Q_r=M_k-B_r^\mathsf{T}M_r^{-1}B_r,
  \qquad
  S_r=M_k^{-1}Q_r
     =I-M_k^{-1}B_r^\mathsf{T}M_r^{-1}B_r.
  \label{eq:ofdg-projection-matrices}
\end{equation}
Thus applying $S_r$ to a degree-$k$ coefficient vector directly returns the
coefficients of the remainder $w-\Pi_K^r w$.  The matrices $M_k$ and $M_r$ are
the degree-$k$ and degree-$r$ mass matrices, respectively; $B_r$ couples the two
bases; and $I$ is the $N_k\times N_k$ identity matrix.  The code stores $S_r$
for $0\leq r\leq k$.

Crucially, this construction never converts the solution to Legendre or other
orthogonal modal coefficients.  It represents the same projection operator in
whatever supported scalar polynomial DG basis the host framework supplies.
Consequently, a nodal basis, a modal basis, or another polynomial coefficient
basis produces the same function-space operation up to quadrature and roundoff.
This basis-agnostic realization is what makes the implementation portable: a
second framework need not add a special OFDG solution basis, provided it can
form the corresponding polynomial-space projections.

On an affine element, all three mass
and coupling matrices contain the same constant physical Jacobian factor, which
cancels in $S_r$.  Therefore the projection matrices are assembled on the first
element and reused on every element even when the element Jacobians differ.

\subsubsection{Coefficient-to-coefficient derivatives}

The repeated derivatives required by~\eqref{eq:ofdg-face-jump} are represented as
polynomial coefficients rather than only as values at quadrature points.  At the
finite element interpolation points $\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_q\}_{q=1}^{N_k}$, define
\begin{equation}
  V_{qi}=\phi_i(\boldsymbol{\xi}_q),
  \qquad
  G_{qi}^{(r)}
  =\frac{\partial\phi_i}{\partial\xi_r}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_q).
\end{equation}
If $\boldsymbol{u}$ is a coefficient vector, $G^{(r)}\boldsymbol{u}$ contains
reference-derivative values at the interpolation points.  The coefficient vector
of that derivative is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{u}_{\xi_r}=D_{\xi_r}\boldsymbol{u},
  \qquad
  D_{\xi_r}=V^{-1}G^{(r)}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-reference-derivative-matrix}
\end{equation}
This Vandermonde construction is kept explicit in the code.  For the affine map
$\boldsymbol{x}=T_K(\boldsymbol{\xi})$, the physical derivative matrices are
\begin{equation}
  D_{x_j}^{K}
  =\sum_{r=1}^d(J_K^{-1})_{rj}D_{\xi_r}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-physical-derivative-matrix}
\end{equation}
The inverse Jacobian $J_K^{-1}$ is read once at the reference-element centre and
one set of matrices $\{D_{x_j}^{K}\}_{j=1}^d$ is stored for every element.

Only first reference derivatives of the supplied finite-element basis are
requested from MFEM.  Repeated application of the resulting coefficient
operators generates every higher derivative needed by OFDG.  In framework
terms, the construction requires scalar polynomial DG basis values and first
derivatives, quadrature, nested lower-order polynomial spaces or equivalent
projection operators, element and face transformations, and local
degree-of-freedom access.  It therefore avoids a modal-basis requirement, but it
does not claim support for arbitrary nonpolynomial, $H(\mathrm{div})$,
$H(\mathrm{curl})$, or mixed finite-element spaces.

All multi-indices with total degree at most $k$ are organized in a directed
acyclic graph.  Each nonzero multi-index selects one parent of degree one less and
one physical derivative direction.  Walking this graph evaluates every required
derivative once.  The number of stored derivative states per element and component
is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{l=0}^k\binom{l+d-1}{d-1}=\binom{k+d}{d}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Face quadrature and cached evaluation}

MFEM remains the sole source of mesh connectivity.  The setup loops over MFEM
face numbers and obtains the two adjacent elements from MFEM's face-transformation
data.  Boundary faces are skipped.  For each
interior face, a quadrature rule of order $2k+1$ is selected for the MFEM face
geometry.  The following data are cached directly at the MFEM face index:
\begin{equation}
  (E_F^-)_{qi}=\phi_i(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{F,q}^-),
  \qquad
  (E_F^+)_{qi}=\phi_i(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{F,q}^+),
  \qquad
  \widehat{\omega}_q=\frac{\omega_q}{\sum_p\omega_p}.
  \label{eq:ofdg-face-evaluation-matrices}
\end{equation}
For affine faces the physical face Jacobian is constant and cancels between the
integral and $|F|$ in~\eqref{eq:ofdg-face-jump}; hence the normalized reference
weights $\widehat{\omega}_q$ are sufficient.  All derivative traces and all
components on one side of a face are evaluated together in one dense matrix
multiplication.  There is no face-pattern matching or deduplication.

If $\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^-$ and
$\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^+$ are the coefficient vectors of the
same physical derivative on the two neighboring elements, the actual quadrature
calculation is
\begin{equation}
  J_{F,c}^{(l)}
  \approx\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=l}\sum_{q=1}^{N_{q,F}}
  \widehat{\omega}_q
  \left|
    \bigl(E_F^-\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^-\bigr)_q
    -\bigl(E_F^+\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^+\bigr)_q
  \right|.
  \label{eq:ofdg-discrete-face-jump}
\end{equation}
The implementation therefore performs one subtraction and one absolute value for
each derivative, component, and quadrature point.  No squared-jump accumulator or
final square root is required.

\subsubsection{Runtime sequence and storage policy}

For every input state, the implementation performs the following operations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item evaluate the global mean and scale~\eqref{eq:ofdg-global-scale} at volume
        quadrature points;
  \item rebuild and store all derivative coefficient states on every element;
  \item loop over MFEM interior faces, evaluate the normal wave speeds and the
        mean absolute jump measures~\eqref{eq:ofdg-face-jump}, and accumulate the rates
        \eqref{eq:ofdg-damping-rate} on both neighboring elements;
  \item apply either the coefficient-space damping operator
        \eqref{eq:ofdg-damping-operator} or the exact decay map
        \eqref{eq:ofdg-exact-decay}.
\end{enumerate}
The volume mean is integrated with a rule of order $2k+1$.  The extrema used for
$A_c$ are the minimum and maximum over the same quadrature values.  In floating
point arithmetic, the implementation sets the rates of component $c$ to zero when
$A_c\leq 10^{-14}$.

There is no bounded or dynamic derivative cache.  Every element owns permanent
storage for its derivative coefficients, and all of it is rebuilt once for the
current state.  Likewise, each interior face owns its two evaluation matrices and
normalized quadrature weights.  Ignoring small container overheads, the principal
storage costs are
\begin{align}
  &\mathcal{O}\left(
    |\mathcal{T}_h|\,dN_k^2
    \right)
  &&\text{for physical derivative operators},\\
  &\mathcal{O}\left(
    |\mathcal{T}_h|\,mN_k\binom{k+d}{d}
    \right)
  &&\text{for derivative coefficient states},\\
  &\mathcal{O}\left(
    \sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_h^{\mathrm{i}}}N_{q,F}(2N_k+1)
    \right)
  &&\text{for face evaluation data}.
\end{align}
This policy uses more predictable memory than an evicting cache and removes cache
lookups and replacement decisions from the face loop.

An optional active-element mask can restrict stabilization or decay to selected
elements.  A face is omitted only when neither neighbor is active, so an active
element still receives contributions from every adjacent interior face. Runtime
scratch arrays are owned by the OFDG object. Because this scratch state is
mutable and reused, concurrent calls on the same object are unsupported.

\subsubsection{Relation to the published formulations}
\label{sec:ofdg-relation-to-literature}

The original multidimensional OFDG sensors in~\cite{LuEtAl2021,LiuEtAl2022}
measure derivative jumps at element vertices.  The system formulation
in~\cite{LiuEtAl2022} additionally transforms jumps to characteristic variables
and takes a maximum over characteristic components.  The current implementation
instead uses the face-averaged absolute-jump measure~\eqref{eq:ofdg-face-jump}
and constructs a
separate rate for each physical component.  Its global amplitude normalization,
normal wave-speed factor, and exact decay map are motivated by the later OEDG
formulation~\cite{PengEtAl2024}.

Consequently, the implementation should be regarded as the specific hybrid OFDG
and OEDG damping variant defined by \cref{eq:ofdg-face-jump},
\cref{eq:ofdg-damping-rate}, and \cref{eq:ofdg-damping-operator}, not as a literal
reproduction of one published algorithm.  Conservation and dissipation follow
directly from \cref{eq:ofdg-local-conservation} and
\cref{eq:ofdg-dissipation}; convergence results proved for the published
vertex-based or characteristic-variable sensors do not, by themselves, constitute
a proof for this modified sensor.

The KXRCF troubled-cell indicator supplies the optional active-element set used
by this mask interface.  Its definition, normalization, and system coupling are
given in \cref{chap:kxrcf}.
